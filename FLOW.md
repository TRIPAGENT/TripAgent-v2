# The flow, and why it is shaped this way

Written against the brief: *a member arrives at a door, opens it with a key, and
from then on the app navigates and plans for them.* The reference point is a
private-membership concierge — Quintessentially and its peers — not a booking site.

## What the research changes

Four findings, and what each one costs us if we ignore it.

**1. They no longer browse.** The 2026 research on where wealthy travellers get
ideas is blunt: not magazines in January, not the July best-of lists, not
Instagram villa references. They ask an assistant. *So the AI is not a support
feature bolted to a catalogue — it is the front door of the planning experience,
and the catalogue is what it reaches for.* We already made the 110 destinations a
tool the agent calls rather than a grid the member is left to work through.

**2. Anticipation is the product.** The defining skill of a 2026 luxury advisor is
predicting a preference before the client voices it. *So the app should arrive
with a proposal, not a form.* A member who has saved three places should be met
with "shall I plan around these?" — which is what the desk now does — never an
empty box and a blinking cursor.

**3. Anti-crowd is the new status symbol.** Silence, space and the absence of
others now outrank the trophy table. *So credentials are evidence, not the pitch.*
A Michelin key tells you the room is serious; it is not the reason to go. The
guide copy leads with what a place is like, and the award sits underneath as a
badge.

**4. Access, not inventory.** Membership buys the unlisted room, the upgrade, the
table that is not taking bookings. *So the app must never present something as
held when it is not.* This is why every price is absent until Tripsure quotes it,
and why the plan page says "Not checked" rather than inventing a number.

## The door

`/signin` — eight characters, nothing else. It fails closed: a well-formed code
that is not on file is refused, because once a payment instrument is on a member
record, letting a stranger self-declare a name is a fraud path with no upside.

The code *is* the identity. It carries the member's initials, resolves to their
file, and becomes the traveller id the agent keys its memory to — so the desk
picks up where it left off, on any device.

## The five rooms beyond it

| Room | What it is for | The one action |
|---|---|---|
| **Explore** | Arriving with no fixed idea | Say where, or pick a month |
| **City** | Deciding within a destination | Save an address |
| **Map** | Being *at* the destination | Find what is near, save it |
| **Wishlist** | What they have kept | Hand it to the desk |
| **Desk** | Planning, and everything after | Ask |

Every room ends in the desk. That is deliberate: the concierge is the product, and
each screen is a way of arriving there with more context than the last.

### Explore — sell the idea, then narrow

The first screen sells nothing but the idea: the brand's own lines over its own
photography, rotating, with one action beneath — *where do you want to go?* No
months, no grid, nothing to read. A member who knows their destination types it
and is gone in two taps.

Below that, the decision aid for members who do not: month first, then the
destinations at their peak in it, each card carrying the two facts that decide
feasibility — **flight time from India and the visa**. Then the three things we
actually handle. The twelve months appear once more as a rail, not a second grid.

### City — the guide leads

Hero, the four facts, then straight into **where to stay / what to do / where to
eat / after dark**, grouped by tier, each entry carrying its credentials and a save
control. The editorial take is last, because it is the least actionable thing on
the page. Nothing here is more than a few lines.

### Map — for when they have arrived

8,069 curated addresses across the catalogue, with coordinates, filtered by
category. Tap a pin, see what it is and what it has earned, save it or add it to
the list. This is the screen a member opens standing on a street in Kyoto, and it
is the natural home for the confirmed itinerary once a trip is booked — the day's
plan, on the ground, in place.

### Desk — where it all lands

Opens with a proposal drawn from what they have kept, not a blank box. Streams,
so a plan build is watchable rather than a dead screen. Hands back a page they can
open and forward, in this app's own design.

## What is deliberately absent

- **No prices anywhere yet.** Until Tripsure quotes, a number would be invented.
- **No named advisor.** There is a desk, and it answers. A stock portrait and a
  fictional name would be a claim about staffing we cannot make.
- **No badge that asserts a sync, hold or verification that has not happened.**
- **No second itinerary.** The desk authors the plan; the app shows the shortlist
  that goes into it.

## The gap that still matters most

The member can plan completely and cannot yet pay. Everything from the quote
onward — Tripsure, the checkout, issuance, the refund rail — is blocked on the
ticketing question in the integration decisions, and that is the next thing worth
settling. A concierge app that cannot close is a very good brochure.
